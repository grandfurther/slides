The pipeline wasn’t empty. The offer was.

Across technical B2B firms in Singapore, the same pattern repeats: buyers find the website, read the first screen, and leave without a reason to reply. No new tool fixes that.

By Chris Basil, founder & editorSingapore4 min read

The demo went well. The dashboard looked busy. Six months later, the sales director at a specialist engineering supplier had the same number of qualified enquiries as before: four a month.

This paper hears versions of that story every week. A B2B firm senses that marketing isn’t producing pipeline, and the market answers with a product — a new CRM, an AI SDR, an automation suite, a 12-month retainer.

No tool can rescue an offer the buyer doesn’t understand.

The evidence usually points somewhere else. The website appears in Google 12,000 times a month and gets clicked 110 times. The enquiry form gets a reply in two days. Outbound emails ask for 20 minutes before giving the buyer anything worth 20 seconds.

Each of those can be measured in an afternoon. That is the job of this paper: find the leak, show the numbers behind it, then decide whether a tool is needed at all.

Case files: four people who will sell you a fix you don’t need

If one is on your payroll, ask the question at the bottom of their file.
FILE 01

The retainer bandit

Method
A 12-month contract and a monthly report full of activity. Results are always one quarter away.
Evidence to request
Qualified meetings per month since the contract started.

Ask them“What happens if I cancel in month three?”

FILE 02

The vanity metric peddler

Method
Reach, impressions, and engagement in large green numbers. Revenue is never on the slide.
Evidence to request
The list of meetings the campaign produced, with company names.

Ask them“What did each qualified meeting cost?”

FILE 03

The list blaster

Method
A bought list, a template, and volume. Every contact gets the same email, whatever their role or reason to buy.
Evidence to request
How each contact was chosen, and the reply rate by segment.

Ask them“Why is this person on the list?”

FILE 04

The tool pusher

Method
Every problem needs new software, and the recommended software pays the recommender a commission.
Evidence to request
The problem the tool solves, stated in your own numbers.

Ask them“Do you get paid if I buy this?”

B1

Letters to the editor

Questions from B2B founders and sales leads. Names and firms withheld.

Our agency says our LinkedIn ads are “performing well.” We’ve had two meetings from them in four months. Who do I believe?

— Head of sales, logistics software, Singapore

THE EDITOR: Believe the meetings. Ask the agency for one number: cost per qualified meeting last month, with the list of meetings attached. If they can’t produce the list, they’re reporting on the ads, not on your pipeline.

A vendor wants to sell us an AI SDR that sends 5,000 emails a week. Our whole market is about 800 companies. Good idea?

— Founder, marine services, Jurong

THE EDITOR: Do the arithmetic first. 5,000 emails a week across 800 companies is six emails per company, every week. That isn’t outreach; it’s a spam complaint. Write properly to the 40 people who actually buy.

We have 9,000 contacts in our CRM and almost nobody opens our emails. Should we switch email tools?

— Marketing manager, industrial supplier, Kuala Lumpur

THE EDITOR: Your tool is fine. Read your last three subject lines out loud. If none of them tells the reader what they get, that is the fix. Give before you ask.

Corrections

A case study cited in a recent agency proposal claimed “312% lead growth.” The starting figure was four leads. The new figure is 16.

A software vendor described its product as “set and forget.” Customers report that it was mostly forgotten.

A proposal promised “full-funnel synergy.” We have been unable to confirm what this means.
